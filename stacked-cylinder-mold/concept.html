Stacked Cylinder Mold: PLA case units → a plaster column

The idea: you print PLA casting units and pour plaster straight into them. Each unit turns out one plaster piece, and every piece is a slice of the same cylinder, with flat parting faces. Stacked, they make one uniform column: a footer at the bottom, then section units keyed down into the one below, then a well collar on top for pouring. Every cut height, piece type and print orientation is worked out from the vessel profile by checking for overhangs. The schematics below are generated by that algorithm, running on six forms.

2 · Keys and pours, step by step

A. Who gets which key

Every joint between two pieces works the same way: the piece above has male cones on its bottom face, and the piece below has sockets in its top face. So the end pieces carry one kind of key and every middle piece carries both: male cones underneath, sockets on top. You never choose a gender. Where a piece sits in the stack decides it.

B. PLA forms the opposite shape

Plaster takes the negative of whatever PLA it sets against. A bump on a PLA plate leaves a socket in the plaster. A pocket in a PLA plate fills with plaster and becomes a male cone.

C. Split pieces add a second set

A split piece follows the same top-and-bottom rule as every other piece. Its two halves also key to each other across the flat divider face: half A gets sockets, half B gets male cones. Stack keys sit at 90° / 210° / 330°, so none falls on the divider line.

D. Pour open, then press on a key lid

Pouring through a small port is slow, plaster starts to set inside it, and air gets trapped under the lid. So nothing is poured through a port. You fill the open unit to just above the rim, then press the key lid down onto the wet plaster. The lid's small bleed holes let surplus plaster and air squeeze out until the lid seats on the sleeve rim, so both faces come out flat. Each unit has two plates: the bed plate it stands on and the lid. Their key formers come from rule A + B, so an inverted unit just has its plates swapped, and both routes give the same finished piece.

Bleed holes

Ø3 mm holes through the lid. One goes at the tip of every pocket, because a pocket faces down when the lid is on and would otherwise trap an air bubble where the male cone should form. Two more go between the keys. Each leaves a small plaster nub: scrape it flush, or let the extra socket depth absorb it.

Key size

90° cones, Ø12 × 5 mm. Each socket is 0.3 mm wider and 1 mm deeper than its cone, so a nub or a crumb of plaster can't hold the faces apart. The faces touch flat and the cones only locate. Three keys at 120° seat without rocking. A round form can go together in any of the three positions, and they all work.

Alternative: loose keys

Every face gets sockets only, and you drop loose double cones (printed PLA or Ø10 mm steel balls) into them as you stack. Then every plate is identical and no genders are tracked at all. The cost is small parts you can lose, and a little more play.

E. Well collar and pooling ledge

The collar has vertical walls. Its bore is at least ¼″ (6.35 mm) wider than the lip all round, so a flat, level ring of the piece below shows inside the well. That ring is the top face of the top body piece, the same flat parting face that carries its sockets. While the mold drinks, the slip level in the well drops, and the ledge catches a horizontal clay flange instead of a funnel-shaped spare. You cut the flange at the lip line, then lift the collar off. The sockets stay outside the bore, and the generator warns if they don't.

4 · Ideas that make the system work

  1. Monotonic band slicing. Cut the profile wherever its radius stops growing or stops shrinking: at the belly (max) and at the waist or neck (min). The belly is also the classic parting line, so the cuts land where a mold maker would put them anyway.
  2. Floor at the wide end. Each ring unit is printed and poured with the wider end of its band on the bed. That makes the core support-free and lets the set plaster lift off toward the narrow end. Units in one stack alternate between upright and inverted.
  3. Ring or split, decided by the demold order. A one-piece ring works only if the ware can get out through it: nothing wider above it (ring lifts up) or nothing wider below it (ring slides down). Any other band becomes a split unit: two halves divided through the axis. That always works for a surface of revolution.
  4. Merge neighbouring splits. Split halves are printed lying on their divider face, so the profile has no print overhangs and the band no longer has to be monotonic. Neighbouring split bands merge into one taller split unit, which means fewer pieces and fewer seams.
  5. One cylinder for the whole mold. Ø = 2·rmax + 2·plaster thickness, rounded up to 10 mm. Every piece is a slice of the same column, so one hose clamp or ratchet strap holds the whole stack. The PLA outer wall is a reusable split sleeve: two half-tubes clamped together, printed at standard heights and shared by every unit. A vertical tube wall never has a print overhang.
  6. Why a cylinder pays off. Plaster is equally thick around a round form, so it absorbs water evenly and the ware casts to an even wall. It also needs about 20% less plaster than a square block, with no heavy corners. And a band clamps a round column evenly.
  7. Three keys at 120°. Three 90° cones on the annular parting face, near the outer edge. Three points always seat without rocking. Male cones go under each piece and sockets on top (section 2A). For a patterned (non-round) form, space them 110° / 120° / 130° so only one rotation fits. The 45° cone flanks print without supports.
  8. Pour open, press on a key lid. A unit is a bed plate (with the core printed on it), the sleeve, and a lid. Fill to just above the rim and press the lid on. Surplus plaster and air leave through the bleed holes, and both parting faces come out flat and keyed (section 2D).
  9. Split halves: print lying, pour standing. The insert is a divider plate plus a half-core, printed lying on the divider (support-free). For pouring it stands on a separate bed plate inside a half-sleeve, gets filled and gets a lid like any other unit. To demold, take off the plates and the sleeve, then pull the plaster half sideways off the insert. You print two inserts: A (divider bumps) and B (divider pockets).
  10. A vertical well over a ¼″ ledge. The well collar is a plain vertical bore, at least ¼″ (6.35 mm) wider than the lip all round. The flat top face of the piece below shows through as a ring, and the slip pools on it into a clay flange that you cut off at the lip. The collar's core is a thin slit tube whose edges meet, taped together on the inside. Peel the tape and ease an edge inward and the plaster comes off a zero-draft wall (section 2E).
  11. Strap grooves with 45° ribs. A shallow V-groove around each piece seats a hose clamp or rubber band. The rib that forms it inside the sleeve is a 45° chevron, so it prints on a vertical wall with no support.
  12. Shrinkage and surface. The profile is scaled by 1/(1 − shrink) before slicing. Cores print at 0.12 mm layers (plaster copies layer lines exactly) and get release agent (Murphy's oil soap / petroleum jelly). Flag bands steeper than 70° from vertical, where stair-stepping shows.
  13. Heat budget. Pottery plaster can reach about 45 °C while it sets, and PLA softens around 60 °C. Print walls with ≥4 perimeters and warn on very thick pieces (the footer, big bowls). Offer PETG as the fallback filament.

5 · Six forms, sliced by the algorithm

Sections are cut through the axis, with keys rotated into the section plane. Parameters: cylinder Ø gives plaster ≥ 25 mm at rmax · footer 30 mm below base · foot seam 6 mm · ring unit ≤ 90 mm · split unit ≤ 180 mm · well collar 30 mm with a 12° funnel · minimum draft 1° (a stretch with less than that for over 12 mm is treated as no draft). Profiles are in mold-size mm (after shrinkage).

6 · Demold sequence (any form)

1 Cut the clay flange off the ledge at the lip line and lift the well collar ↑. 2 Lift the ↑ rings from the top down. 3 Open the ⟷ split units sideways. 4 Invert the remaining stack and lift the footer off. 5 Slide the ↓ rings off toward the foot, or lift the ware out of them. This order can't conflict: the geometry rules out a ↓ ring sitting above a ↑ ring, so the sequence is always valid.
